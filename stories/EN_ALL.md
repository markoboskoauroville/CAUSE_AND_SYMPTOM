### A Story for the Children — *The Bell in the Tower*

In the valley there was a tower, and in the tower there was a bell, and the bell had only one job. When the river rose too high, the bell would ring.

It did not ring softly. It rang loud enough to wake the bakers and the babies and the dogs, and everybody in the valley would grumble and pull their blankets over their heads.

One winter the bell rang for three nights running, and on the fourth night a man climbed the tower with an armful of wool and packed it in tight around the clapper.

The valley slept beautifully after that. Everyone said it was the best sleep in years.

Mira was seven, and she slept beautifully too. But in the morning she walked down to look at the river the way she always did, and the river had come further up the bank than she had ever seen it. It was licking the bottom step of the mill.

She ran and found old Vida, who kept the bees, and Vida did not say anything at all for a while. She just walked down and looked at the water with her hands behind her back.

"The bell has been quiet," Mira said.

"Yes," said Vida. "The bell has been very quiet."

"So everything is all right?"

Vida crouched down beside her so their eyes were level. "Mira," she said, "when you cover your ears, does the noise stop, or do you only stop hearing it?"

Mira thought about that.

Then she went and found a ladder, and she climbed the tower, and she pulled out every last piece of wool. And when the bell rang that night, loud enough to wake the bakers and the babies and the dogs, she lay in her bed and she was not annoyed with it at all.

She was glad somebody was still shouting.

### A Story for the Children — *Two Buckets*

Tam was Mira's little brother, and Tam was the fastest runner in the valley, which he mentioned often.

One evening the chimney at the mill caught fire. Sparks came out of the top like a swarm of orange bees, and everybody came running with buckets, and Tam was first, because of course he was.

He got to the mill and he stopped, and he looked up at the chimney, and he said, very seriously, "The mortar between those stones is crumbling. That is why this happened. We should mix new mortar."

Old Vida took the bucket out of his hands, very gently, and threw the water on the fire.

"But the mortar," said Tam.

"Yes," said Vida, and threw another one.

They put the fire out. It took an hour and everybody's arms hurt and the miller cried a little, and then they all sat down on the wall in the dark.

"So I was wrong," said Tam.

"No," said Vida. "You were right. The mortar is crumbling. That is exactly why this happened, and if we do not fix it, it will happen again in the spring."

"Then why did you take my bucket?"

Vida rubbed her sore shoulder. "Because you cannot mix mortar into a burning chimney," she said. "Water first. Mortar after. Not instead of — after."

Tam thought about this for a long time, swinging his legs against the wall.

"How do you know when it is the after part?" he asked.

"When nothing is on fire," said Vida.

In the morning they went back with sand and lime and a trowel, and it was slow and boring and it took four whole days, and nobody clapped for them, and the chimney did not catch fire again for the rest of Tam's life.

### A Story for the Children — *The Splinter*

There was a woman in the valley called Nell, and Nell was very, very fast.

If you came to her with a splinter in your thumb, she would have it out before you finished saying "splinter." If you fell out of the walnut tree and your arm went the wrong way, she would put it right and tie it up and you would be running again by the next moon. When the fever came through the valley one autumn, she went house to house for nine nights without sleeping, and every single child in that valley grew up.

Some people in the valley liked to say clever things about Nell. They said she only fixed the outside. They said she did not understand the deeper way of things.

Mira listened to them, and then she went and asked Vida about it, because Vida understood the deeper way of things and kept bees.

Vida was quiet for a moment. Then she rolled up her sleeve and showed Mira a thin white line on her forearm, old and faded.

"I was nineteen," she said. "There was a scythe, and there was a great deal of blood, and I remember thinking, very calmly, that I was going to die in a hayfield in the sunshine."

"What happened?"

"Nell happened," said Vida. "In about four minutes."

Mira looked at the scar.

"When somebody is bleeding," Vida said, "there is no deeper way of things. There is only Nell. And anyone who tells you otherwise has never watched someone bleed."

She rolled her sleeve back down.

"Fast is not shallow, Mira. Fast is just what you need when the clock is faster than you are."

### A Story for the Children — *The Path to the Spring*

Tam wanted water.

The spring was up the hill, past the stones, through the beech wood, and it was a long way. So every morning Tam waited by the door with the jug, and every morning somebody bigger carried it up and carried it back.

This worked perfectly. Tam had water. Tam had never been to the spring.

Then one summer everyone bigger was busy with the harvest, and Tam stood at the door with his jug and nobody came.

He went himself. He got the path wrong twice. He fell in a nettle patch. He arrived at the spring furious and stung and thirsty, and he sat down in the moss and cried, and then he drank, and then he walked home, and the jug was heavy and his arms shook.

The next day was a little less bad. The day after that he found a shortcut past the fallen beech. By the end of the summer he could do it without thinking about it, singing, with the jug on his shoulder.

Years later, when Tam was a grown man and living over the mountain, someone asked him if he ever got lost in strange country.

"Never," said Tam. "I always find water."

"How?"

And Tam could not really explain it. Because the thing he had been given was not in the jug. It had never been in the jug. It was in his legs and his nose and the way he read a hillside, and nobody could take it off him, and nobody could carry it for him either — which was exactly why it was his.

### A Story for the Children — *The Cold Morning*

When Mira decided to learn to swim in the river, the first morning was horrible.

The water was so cold it took her breath. Her skin burned. Her teeth clacked. She got out after ten seconds and sat on a rock feeling wretched and stupid.

"It is meant to be like this at first," said Vida.

The second morning was horrible too. The fourth was slightly less horrible. By the tenth she could stay in and swim a little, and by the end of the month she came out of that river glowing like a lamp.

Now here is the important part of the story.

That same autumn, a boy called Rook started swimming too. And on his ninth morning Rook came out of the water and he was not shivering. He was hot. His ear hurt and he could not stand up straight and everything looked too bright.

"It is meant to be like this at first," said Rook, because that is what he had heard.

Mira looked at him. Then she went and got Vida, and Vida took one look at Rook and carried him to Nell, and Nell put drops in his ear, and by the next week he was well.

Afterwards Mira asked, "How was I supposed to know? He said the same words I said."

"He did," said Vida. "So here is how you know. Hard is cold, and shaky, and it gets a little easier every day, and it happens right after you did the new thing."

"And the other one?"

"The other one gets worse instead of better. Or it goes on far too long. Or there is something in it that does not belong — a fever, a fright, a pain that grows."

Mira nodded slowly.

"And if you are not sure," said Vida, "you fetch me. Every time. Being wrong about it costs nothing. Being right about it too late costs everything."

### A Story for the Children — *The Bridge Mira Built*

There was a stream between Mira's house and the field where her friends played, and the stream was too wide to jump.

A big kind man called Bo used to carry her over. He would swing her up and stride across and set her down, and she loved it, and it took no time at all.

Then Bo moved to the next valley.

Mira stood on the bank for four days.

On the fifth day she dragged a plank down. It was too short. On the sixth day she found two planks and they slipped. On the seventh day she cried and kicked one of the planks into the water and had to go in after it.

On the eleventh day she built a bridge out of three planks and a great many stones and a piece of rope from the barn, and it was ugly, and it wobbled, and it held.

Her friends came to look at it. One of them said it was not as good as being carried by Bo.

"No," Mira agreed. "It is much worse than being carried by Bo."

Then she crossed it, and came back, and crossed it again, just because she could.

That winter the stream flooded and took her bridge away, and everybody expected Mira to cry.

She did not. She went and got three more planks.

Because the thing she had built the first time was not really the bridge. The bridge had only been made of wood. The other thing — the knowing-how, the hands that had done it once and could do it again — that had been built inside her, and no flood in the world knew where to find it.

### A Story for the Children — *Where the Worry Lives*

Pim was a small creature with a very long tail, and Pim's tail told the truth about Pim whether Pim liked it or not.

When Pim was happy the tail went up in a lovely curl. When Pim was thinking, it swished. And when Pim was frightened, the tail wound itself tight around Pim's own leg and squeezed.

Pim did not like this at all. Pim wanted to be brave. So Pim learned to say "I am not frightened" in a firm clear voice, and Pim said it often.

But the tail kept squeezing, and after a while Pim's leg began to ache all the time, and Pim could not sleep, and Pim could not remember why.

Vida found Pim sitting by the well at midnight rubbing that sore leg.

"Your tail is very tight," she said.

"My tail is fine," said Pim. "I am not frightened."

"I did not ask whether you were frightened," said Vida. "I said your tail is tight."

And Pim looked down, properly, for the first time in months. And there it was, wound round and round, gripping like a fist.

"Oh," said Pim quietly. "It has been doing that a long time."

Vida sat down on the well wall. "Your mouth can say anything it likes," she said. "Your tail only knows how to tell the truth. That is not a fault in your tail. That is what tails are for."

So Pim stopped arguing with the tail and started listening to it instead. When it tightened, Pim would stop, and breathe out very slowly and for a long time, longer than breathing in, and let it loosen on its own.

It took a whole season. But Pim's leg stopped aching, and Pim slept, and Pim was actually braver afterwards than when he was busy saying so.

### A Story for the Children — *The Same Garden*

Mira was lying on her stomach on the forest floor, nose almost in the leaves, watching.

There were pale threads running everywhere under the leaf litter, finer than hair, joining a beech root to a mushroom to another root, spreading out in every direction like a map with no edges.

"What are they?" she asked.

"They feed the trees," said Vida. "And the trees feed them. Nobody is in charge. Everybody is eating and everybody is being eaten and somehow it all stands up."

Mira watched a long time. Then she said, "It looks like the picture in Nell's book. The one of the inside of a person."

Vida smiled. "It does."

"That is just a coincidence," said Mira, who was at the age of being suspicious.

"Perhaps," said Vida. "But think about what you had for supper. Where was it before it was you?"

Mira thought. "The garden."

"And where was the garden before it was the garden?"

"...The rain. And the dirt."

"And the air you are breathing in right now — where was it a minute ago?"

Mira looked up at the beech leaves moving above her.

"In the tree," she said slowly.

"So when I say you are made of this valley," said Vida, "I am not being poetic with you, Mira. I am telling you where the pieces came from. And that is why, when a person is unwell, I never only look at the person. I look at what they have been eating and where they have been sleeping and who they have been sitting with. Because those things are not around them."

"What are they, then?"

"They are them," said Vida. "Only slightly earlier."

### A Story for the Children — *The Naming*

Something began following Rook.

He could not have told you what it looked like. It was behind him in the lane, and it sat on his chest at night, and it was there when he woke up, and the worst part was that when his mother asked him what was wrong he could only say "nothing," because it truly had no shape and no name and nothing to point at.

A thing with no name is very hard to be brave about.

So his mother took him to Vida, and Vida did not tell him it was nothing. She did not tell him to be a big boy. She got out a piece of charcoal and a flat white stone.

"Draw it," she said.

"I can't. It doesn't look like anything."

"Then draw the wrong shape," said Vida. "It will still be closer than no shape at all."

Rook drew a smudge with too many legs. Then he looked at it and shook his head and drew it again, lower and wider and heavier, and stopped.

"That's it," he said, surprised.

"Good," said Vida. "Now it has a shape. What is it called?"

Rook thought for a long time. "The Weight," he said.

That evening Vida asked the village to come and sit in a ring in the yard, which they did, because that is what that valley did. Rook stood in the middle and held up the stone and said, "This is the Weight. It has been following me since the spring."

Nobody laughed. His uncle said, "I had one of those." A woman from the mill said, "Mine had wings." Someone put a blanket round his shoulders.

The Weight did not vanish. It got smaller, and it got a name, and it got carried by more than one person, and those three things were nearly the same thing.

### A Story for the Children — *The Field of One Flower*

The new farmer in the valley loved yellow flowers, and he loved them so much that he pulled up everything else.

No hedges. No thistles. No poppies, no clover, no nettle patch at the bottom by the gate. Just one enormous field of one yellow flower, perfectly even, from the wall to the river, and it was the most beautiful thing anybody had ever seen.

The beetles could not believe their luck.

They came in the second summer. To a beetle, that field was not a field. It was a table set for a wedding, laid end to end with the only food it had ever wanted, and nothing anywhere to eat the beetle.

The farmer sprayed. The beetles went. He was very pleased.

They came back the next year, and there were more of them, and the ladybirds who used to eat them did not come back at all, because there was nowhere left for a ladybird to live.

By the fourth summer the farmer sat down on his wall and put his head in his hands.

Mira, who was eleven by then and rude, said, "You could put the nettles back."

He laughed at her.

But in the autumn she saw him planting a hedge along the north edge — hawthorn and dog rose and a mess of other things — and leaving the corner by the gate to go wild, and digging a small ugly pond.

It looked far worse. His neighbours said so.

The next summer there were ladybirds in the hedge and frogs in the ugly pond and beetles in the field, plenty of them, but never so many that anyone had to panic.

"It is not as tidy," the farmer admitted.

"No," said Mira. "But something is eating everything now. That is what tidy was missing."

### A Story for the Children — *The River Who Was Made Straight*

The river through the valley did not go in a straight line. It wandered. It looped round the meadow, doubled back at the willows, spread out shallow and slow over the gravel where the children caught minnows, and in spring it went quietly over its banks into the low field and sat there for a week like a lake, and then went home again.

The people downstream in the town got tired of waiting for it.

So they came with machines and made the river straight. They cut off the loops, they walled the banks in stone, they filled in the low field and built houses on it, and the water went from the mountain to the town in a quarter of the time.

Everyone said it was much improved.

Then came the year of the long rain.

The river had nowhere to spread and nothing to slow it. It arrived at the town all at once, in a hurry, exactly as it had been taught, and it went through the streets.

So they built the walls higher. And the next time it went over the higher walls. So they built them higher again.

Mira went with Vida to look at the town, years later. The walls were enormous now, grey and cold, and you could not see the water from the street at all. The children there had never caught a minnow.

"Could it be put back?" Mira asked.

"Some of it," said Vida. "Not the low field — there are houses on it now. But the loops, up in our valley. The willows. The gravel."

"Would that help the town?"

"More than the walls ever will," said Vida. "Because a river that is allowed to dawdle upstream arrives downstream politely. And a river that is hurried arrives like a fist."

### A Story for the Children — *The Well on the Hill*

There were two villages, one at the top of the hill and one at the bottom, and one well between them, up at the top.

For a long time it worked like this: the lower village sent up grain and wool and a boy to help with the winding, and the upper village sent down water, and everybody grumbled about the arrangement in the ordinary way, and everybody had enough.

Then a new headman came to the upper village and worked something out. The water was theirs. It came out of their hill. Why send so much down? He took the grain and the wool and the boy, and he sent down a quarter of the water.

The lower village got thirsty. Then their animals got thin. Then, one family at a time, they packed up and walked up the hill and sat outside the upper village's gate with all their belongings, because that is where the water was.

The upper village was furious. There were people at the gate!

Half of them said, let them in, they are thirsty. The other half said, build the gate higher.

They argued about the gate for two whole years, and while they argued, more people arrived, and nobody once walked up to look at the well.

Mira was visiting her cousin there, and she was eleven, and she said the thing children say.

"They came because you kept the water."

The room went very quiet.

"That is not the point," said the headman.

"It is the whole point," said Mira. "You can build the gate as high as the sky. They will still be thirsty at the bottom of it."

It took another four years and a great deal of shouting, but in the end they went back to grain and wool and a boy for the winding, and water going down.

And the gate stayed where it was, at the ordinary height, and after a while nobody stood outside it at all.

### A Story for the Children — *The Boundary Stone*

Two brothers had two orchards side by side, and between them, sunk in the grass, was a flat boundary stone that their grandfather had put there.

One dry spring, the older brother moved it. Only a little. Two hands' width into his brother's ground, where the best apple tree stood.

The younger brother noticed in the autumn. He did not say anything about the stone. He said something about a gate. Then something about a dog. Then he threw a windfall apple over the hedge, and his brother threw one back, and by winter they were throwing stones, and by the second year the children were throwing stones, and nobody in either house could have told you how it started.

The valley tried to help. They made the brothers agree to stop throwing for one week. It worked, and then it stopped working. They agreed again for a month. It worked, and then it stopped working. Every time it started again it started angrier.

At last Vida walked up between the two orchards with a spade and stood in the wet grass and said, "I am going to look at the stone."

The older brother went white.

"I am not going to say who is bad," said Vida. "I am too old and it is too cold. I am going to find out where the stone should be, and put it there, and then we can all go and have soup."

It took the whole afternoon and there was a great deal of arguing about the grandfather's markings. But the stone went back.

The throwing did not stop that day. It stopped the following spring, slowly, the way frost goes.

Later Mira asked why the weeks of not-throwing had never held.

"Because stopping the throwing is not peace," said Vida. "It is only quiet. And quiet is worth having — people get hurt in the throwing, so you stop it first, always. But quiet with the stone still in the wrong place is just the next fight, waiting."

### A Story for the Children — *The Same Shape*

Mira had reached the age of noticing things, which is a very difficult age.

She noticed that the little whirlpool in the stream by the mill went round the same way as the seedhead of a sunflower.

She noticed that the veins in a beech leaf looked like the streams running down the valley when you stood high up on the ridge.

She noticed that the bell in the tower, the nettles in the farmer's field, the wall around the town, the gate on the hill, and the two brothers throwing apples were, somehow, when you squinted, all the same story.

She found this maddening, and she said so.

"Why is everything the same shape?" she demanded. "It is not magic, is it? Please say it is not magic."

Vida laughed properly, which she rarely did.

"It is not magic," she said. "It is much better than magic. It is because all of those things are made of parts that answer each other."

"Answer each other?"

"The water pushes the bank and the bank pushes the water back. The beetle eats the flower and the ladybird eats the beetle. The brother throws and the brother throws. When things answer each other in a ring, they end up making the same shapes, whatever they happen to be made of. A river does not know it is behaving like a quarrel. It just is."

Mira sat with this for a while.

"So if I learn how one of them works —"

"— then you have learned a little bit about all of them," said Vida. "Yes. That is why an old woman with bees is allowed to have an opinion about a town wall."

### A Story for the Children — *Grandmother's Spoon*

In Nell's cupboard there was a small brown bottle, and in the bottle there was a bitter syrup, and Nell was very careful about it.

One spoon, for a child with a hard cough, meant a night of sleep instead of a night of coughing.

Tam once asked why she did not simply give ten spoons, and cure the cough ten times as fast.

"Because ten spoons would stop the cough forever," said Nell, "along with the child."

Tam did not ask again.

But he thought about it for years, and he noticed that it was true of nearly everything.

One log makes a fire. Twenty logs make a house fire.

A little salt makes the soup. A lot of salt makes it uneatable.

Sleeping late on a cold Sunday is one of the best things there is. Sleeping late every day of your life is a sadness.

The wall the town built was a good wall when it was as high as a person and a terrible wall when it was as high as a house.

And Tam, who had grown up to be a slow and careful man, used to say a thing that annoyed people at meetings.

They would ask him, "Is this good or is this bad?" — about a rule, or a medicine, or a machine — and Tam would always answer the same way.

"How much," he would say, "and for how long?"

And they would groan, because they wanted a shorter answer.

But there wasn't one.

### A Story for the Children — *Mira's Question*

When Mira was grown and Vida was very old, people started coming to Mira with their troubles, the way they had always come to Vida.

She was not clever, exactly. She only had one question, and she asked it every single time, and it drove people mad.

A man came and said his roof kept leaking, he had patched it eleven times.

Mira said: "What has to keep happening for it to leak?"

He said the rain. She said the rain falls on every roof in the valley and only yours leaks. He went away cross. He came back three weeks later and said the gutter above it was blocked, and the water had been running down the wall for six years, and he had been patching the wrong end of the water the whole time.

A woman came and said she was tired all the time.

Mira said: "What has to keep happening for you to be tired?"

The woman began to say "nothing," and then stopped, and sat down, and said that her brother's boy came at ten every night and stayed until two.

A boy came and said the other children would not play with him.

Mira did not ask him the question, because he was eight and crying. She gave him bread and honey and sat with him until he stopped. Then, the next day, she asked it.

That was the other half of it, and she had learned that from Vida too.

Ask what keeps happening. Follow it back until you reach the first thing you can actually put your hand on. Change one thing, and watch, and count.

But not while the child is crying.
First the bread and the honey.
Then the question.

### A Story for the Children — *The Last Story: What Mira Told the Children*

When Mira was as old as Vida had been, the children of the valley used to come and sit on her wall in the evening, and one of them always asked the same thing.

"How do you know when something is really being fixed?"

And Mira would say: "You listen for the counting."

They never understood, so she would explain it the same way every time.

"When a thing is truly getting better, you need less of the fixing as the years go by. Less medicine. Less shouting. Less spraying. Less wall. The doing gets smaller because the trouble underneath is getting smaller.

"And when a thing is only being covered up, you need more. More medicine. More shouting. More wall. Every year a little more, to stand still in the same place.

"So when someone tells you a thing is working, do not look at how it feels today. Count how much they needed last year, and how much they need now. The count will tell you the truth even when nobody in the room will."

Then, usually, one of the children would ask whether that meant the fast fixes were bad.

And Mira would think of Nell's hands, and the fire at the mill, and the boy pulled out of the flood at Whit, and she would shake her head hard.

"Never," she would say. "Never once. Put the fire out. Pull the splinter. Catch the child. Do it fast and do not apologise for it.

"Only — " and here she would point at them with one finger, the way Vida used to — "do not stop there. Because the fire always started somewhere, and the somewhere is still there in the morning."
